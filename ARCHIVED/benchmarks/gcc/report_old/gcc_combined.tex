\begin{abstract}
This report presents the register spill characterisation of
\texttt{502.gcc\_r} across all three SPEC CPU2017 input sizes
(test, train, refrate) executed on RISC-V (RV64GC) using the
gem5 TimingSimpleCPU simulator with a custom SpillDetector.
Spill rates are \textbf{6.92\%} (test, 1 file), \textbf{8.12\%}
(train, 3 files combined), and \textbf{$\approx$7.4\%} (refrate,
3 of 5 files completed). The train input is the highest across
all 17 evaluated SPEC CPU2017 benchmarks. Two-thirds of store
traffic and one-third of load traffic in the train ROI are
spill-related, confirming that \texttt{gcc\_r} is the most
register-pressure-intensive workload in the suite on RISC-V.
\end{abstract}

% =========================================================
\section{Benchmark Overview}

\texttt{502.gcc\_r} is the SPEC CPU2017 rate variant of GCC~4.3.2.
It executes a RISC-V binary of GCC that produces \textbf{x86}
assembly output: the compiler itself runs on RISC-V inside gem5,
but targets x86. All register pressure measured by the SpillDetector
arises from the RISC-V execution of the compiler.

ROI markers were inserted around the \texttt{toplev\_main(argc, argv)}
call in \texttt{src/main.c}, encompassing the complete compilation
pipeline:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{m5\_work\_begin(0,0)} --- before \texttt{toplev\_main()}
    \item \texttt{m5\_work\_end(0,0)}   --- after  \texttt{toplev\_main()}
\end{itemize}

\section{Test: Simulation Setup}

The test input \texttt{t1.c} (a minimal \texttt{printf("hello world")}
program) is compiled with \texttt{-O3 -finline-limit=50000}. Despite
the trivial source, GCC's startup, option processing, and back-end
initialisation all execute in full.

\begin{verbatim}
./build/RISCV/gem5.opt
  --outdir=benchmarks/gcc/m5out_test
  configs/deprecated/example/se.py
  --cmd=benchmarks/gcc/gcc_r.riscv
  --options="benchmarks/gcc/t1.c
    -O3 -finline-limit=50000
    -o benchmarks/gcc/t1.s"
  --cpu-type=TimingSimpleCPU
  --caches --mem-size=4GB
\end{verbatim}

\section{Test: Analysis}

The spill rate of \textbf{6.92\%} is the third highest across all
evaluated benchmarks (behind \texttt{cactuBSSN\_r} at 7.88\% and
\texttt{perlbench\_r} at 7.28\%). The spill-to-load ratio of
\textbf{37.5\%} is the highest in the study: nearly 2 in 5 loads
is a spill reload. The test input exercises only a narrow subset of
GCC's passes, yet the fixed per-compilation overhead produces severe
register pressure even for a 69-byte source file.

\section{Train: Analysis}

The combined spill rate of \textbf{8.12\%} is the highest of all
17 evaluated benchmarks. The 1.95:1 load-to-store ratio reflects
GCC's IR-traversal pattern: GCC reads pointer fields far more often
than it writes them. Two-thirds (66.7\%) of all stores are spill
writes, and nearly half (45.2\%) of all memory operations in the
ROI are spill-related --- the dominant form of memory activity.

Compared to test, the spill rate rises from 6.92\% to 8.12\%
(+17.3\%), while the spill/load fraction drops from 37.5\% to
34.2\%. The larger source files activate the full GIMPLE and RTL
optimisation pipelines, increasing total memory traffic faster
than spill count grows.

ROI coverage: $220{,}789{,}486{,}676 / 220{,}801{,}545{,}416
\approx \mathbf{99.99\%}$.

\section{Ref: Analysis}

Across the three completed ref runs, the spill rate ranges from
6.75\% (\texttt{ref\_smaller} with \texttt{-O3 -fipa-pta}) to
7.99\% (\texttt{ref\_pp\_O3} with \texttt{-O3 -finline-limit=0}).
Disabling inlining (\texttt{-finline-limit=0}) produces the highest
spill rate by preventing GCC from collapsing call chains, keeping more
live variables simultaneously active across more function boundaries.
By contrast, \texttt{-fipa-pta} enables interprocedural pointer
analysis, which reduces aliasing uncertainty and allows better register
allocation, resulting in the lowest spill rate of the set.

The ref spill/store ratios ($\approx$56--64\%) are consistent with
train (66.7\%) and the 61.6\% partial average confirms the pattern.
The ref spill/load ratio (28.96\% partial) is slightly lower than
train (34.19\%), reflecting larger input files that generate more
genuine IR data-load traffic relative to spill reloads.

\section*{Cross-Input Summary}

The spill rate rises from test to train because the larger source
files activate more GCC optimisation passes and longer call chains.
The ref partial result falls between test and train. Across all
input sizes the spill/store ratio (57--67\%) significantly exceeds
the spill/load ratio (29--37\%), confirming that stack writes dominate
the store stream while spill reloads are diluted by the large volume
of genuine IR pointer reads.
